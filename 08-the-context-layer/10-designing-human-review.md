# Designing Human Review
{: .no_toc }

*Video duration: 19:50*

Every prior lecture in this module has been building toward a single recurring idea: sometimes the right move is for the agent to stop and hand the decision to a human. It's easy to treat that as a workflow concern — a checkbox in a pipeline diagram labeled "human approval" — and the Workflow Layer module does cover review gates from that angle. But a human review checkpoint is also, fundamentally, a context management decision, because the entire value of the checkpoint depends on one question this module keeps returning to: what does the reviewer actually see at the moment they're asked to decide? Get that wrong, and the checkpoint exists on paper but doesn't function in practice.

## The checkpoint is only as good as its context

A review gate that hands a human an ambiguous data dump and asks "does this look right?" isn't meaningfully safer than no gate at all — it just relocates the burden of assembling and interpreting context from the agent to an overworked, time-pressured human who now has to do that work under worse conditions than the agent had, usually without the same tooling to search or cross-reference. Conversely, a review gate that hands a human three carefully chosen facts and a clear, specific question can produce a fast, well-informed decision that meaningfully catches errors the agent couldn't catch on its own. The difference between those two outcomes isn't whether a human is in the loop; it's whether the context handed to that human was designed with the same rigor as the context handed to the agent itself.

This reframes the whole topic usefully: designing human review is not a separate skill from the context management covered in the rest of this module — it's the same skill, applied to a different reader. Everything already covered about signal dilution, provenance, and appropriately granular options applies just as much to a human reviewer's context as it does to an agent's.

## When the model should stop and defer

The first design question is *when* a checkpoint belongs in a system at all, and this connects directly to the escalation lecture earlier in the module. A checkpoint earns its place at a decision point where at least one of two conditions holds: the decision is high-stakes or hard to reverse, or the agent's confidence in its own read of the situation is genuinely low. Neither condition alone always justifies a stop — a low-stakes decision made with low confidence is often fine to make and flag, as the escalation lecture covered — but the combination, or either condition at an extreme, is where a human checkpoint earns its cost.

It's worth being explicit that a checkpoint has a real cost, not just a benefit. Every stop-and-wait introduces latency, consumes a human's limited attention, and — if triggered too often or on decisions that didn't actually need it — degrades the quality of every future review by training the reviewer to skim. This is the human-review version of the over-escalation problem discussed earlier: a system that routes every decision, however minor, through human review isn't cautious, it's just expensive and eventually ignored. The architect's job is to place checkpoints where they earn their cost and nowhere else, which means being able to articulate, for each candidate checkpoint, specifically what kind of error it's meant to catch and why an agent operating alone couldn't reliably catch it itself.

A useful test: if you can't state what specific failure mode a checkpoint exists to catch, it's probably in the wrong place, or redundant with a check that already exists elsewhere in the system. "Have a human review this because it seems important" is not a design; "have a human review this because the agent's confidence in the vendor match is below a threshold, and a wrong match here triggers a payment that's expensive to reverse" is.

## What belongs in a review checkpoint's context

Once you've decided a checkpoint belongs at a given point, the harder and more context-management-specific question is what to actually show the reviewer. Three principles do most of the work here.

**Show the decision, not the whole session.** A human being asked to approve a refund doesn't need the full transcript of the twenty-turn conversation that led up to it — they need the specific facts that bear on the specific decision: the customer's claim, the account history relevant to eligibility, the calculated amount, and the policy basis for that calculation. This is a direct application of the signal-dilution principle from earlier in this module: a reviewer buried in a long, unfiltered transcript is exactly as likely to miss the one detail that matters as a model would be, and arguably more likely, because humans reviewing agent output under time pressure tend to skim even harder than a careful re-reading would require.

**Surface uncertainty and provenance explicitly, don't bury it in prose.** If the checkpoint exists specifically because the agent's confidence was low on some point, that point needs to be the most visible thing in the reviewer's context, not a caveat mentioned in passing in paragraph three of a summary. The best-designed checkpoints separate what's known confidently from what's uncertain as a matter of structure — a distinct field or flagged line for "this is the part I'm unsure about and why" — rather than relying on a reviewer to notice a hedge word buried in otherwise confident-sounding prose. This is provenance tracking, covered fully two lectures ahead, applied directly to the review-checkpoint use case: a reviewer needs to know not just what the agent concluded, but how it got there and how sure it was.

**Give the reviewer an appropriately granular set of responses, not just a yes/no gate.** The granularity principle from earlier in this module applies to human decision points every bit as much as it applies to agent ones. A checkpoint that only offers "approve" or "reject" forces a binary choice even when the honest answer is "approve the first two line items, flag the third for more information." Designing the response options as granularly as the decision actually requires — including an explicit "I need more information before I can decide" option — keeps the checkpoint from silently pressuring a rushed reviewer toward whichever binary choice feels less effortful in the moment.

## The failure mode of too much context

It's worth dwelling on a mistake that comes from good intentions: giving a reviewer *everything*, on the theory that more information can only help a human make a better-informed call. This instinct is understandable — nobody wants to be accused of hiding information from a reviewer — but it produces exactly the same signal-dilution failure discussed throughout this module, just relocated to a human reader instead of a model. A reviewer handed forty screens of raw data to approve a single decision will, in practice, either take far longer than the checkpoint's value justifies, or — far more commonly under real time pressure — skim past most of it and approve based on a shallow read that the volume of material actively discouraged from being thorough.

The discipline here is the same editorial judgment that governs good summarization elsewhere in this module: identifying what's actually load-bearing for *this specific decision* and presenting that, with a clear path to the underlying detail for a reviewer who wants to dig deeper, rather than forcing every reviewer to wade through the full detail by default. A well-designed checkpoint interface often looks like a concise summary up front, with the full supporting detail available on demand rather than pushed onto the reviewer unconditionally — the reviewer gets to choose their own depth, rather than having a fixed depth chosen for them by the volume of what was included.

## Designing for a fast, correct "no"

A subtlety that's easy to overlook: a good review checkpoint has to make rejecting or flagging just as easy as approving, not just easier to click through toward approval. Interfaces and prompts that make "approve" the path of least resistance — a single confirming action versus a form requiring detailed justification to reject — systematically bias reviewers toward approval regardless of whether it's warranted, especially under the routine fatigue of reviewing many similar decisions in sequence. If eighteen consecutive checkpoints have all been correct, and rejecting requires meaningfully more effort than approving, a reviewer's actual behavior will drift toward rubber-stamping well before their stated judgment would. Designing the review interaction itself — not just its content — with that asymmetry in mind is part of what separates a checkpoint that functions from one that exists only nominally.

## Checkpoints as part of the larger context strategy

It's worth closing by tying this back to the module's throughline. A human review checkpoint is, in the end, just another consumer of context — one that happens to be a person rather than a model, with different strengths (genuine judgment, accountability, real-world knowledge outside the system) and different weaknesses (limited time and attention, susceptibility to fatigue and interface-driven bias) than an agent has. Everything this module has covered about scoping context deliberately, marking uncertainty explicitly, preserving provenance, and offering appropriately granular options applies to designing for that reader exactly as much as it applies to designing for the model. The systems that get human review right treat the reviewer's context window — their attention, not a token budget, but a real and equally finite resource — with the same discipline the rest of this module asks you to bring to the model's.

## A worked example: designing one checkpoint end to end

It's useful to walk through the design of a single checkpoint from scratch, because the principles above are easier to apply in sequence than to hold in mind all at once. Suppose an agent handles vendor payment approvals up to a threshold, and anything above that threshold, or anything where its confidence in the vendor match is low, routes to a human.

Start with the "when" question: what specific failure is this checkpoint meant to catch? Here it's two distinct risks bundled into one gate — a large payment being wrong in a way that's expensive to reverse, and a vendor mismatch that could send money to the wrong party entirely. Naming those two risks separately matters, because it shapes what the checkpoint needs to show; a gate designed only around "is this amount right" would miss surfacing the vendor-match uncertainty clearly enough for a reviewer to catch it.

Next, decide what belongs in the reviewer's context. Not the full session — not the twelve tool calls the agent made gathering vendor history, not the two rejected earlier drafts of the payment record. What the reviewer actually needs is: the vendor name and its match confidence against the system of record, the invoice amount and what it's for, and — critically, given the two risks just named — an explicit, separated field for "why this needs review" rather than folding that reasoning into a general summary. If the match confidence is low because two vendors have nearly identical names, that specific fact needs to be the most visible thing on the screen, not a detail a reviewer has to infer from a paragraph of otherwise routine-sounding text.

Then design the response options. A bare approve/reject gate forces a binary choice even though the real decision space is richer: approve as submitted, approve but flag the vendor record for a data-quality fix, reject and request clarification on the amount, or reject because the vendor match is wrong entirely. Building those as genuinely distinct options — not just an approve/reject toggle with a free-text comment box nobody reads later — means the system captures *which* of the two original risks actually triggered a problem when one does, which in turn is exactly the kind of provenance that would let you later analyze whether this checkpoint is catching real errors or mostly noise.

Finally, consider the interaction design itself: is approving meaningfully easier than rejecting? If approving is one click and rejecting requires a written justification, then under routine load — most vendor payments being unremarkable — reviewer behavior will drift toward approval regardless of the specific case in front of them. Matching the friction of both paths, or if anything making the higher-risk path (approving something flagged for a *vendor match* concern specifically) slightly more deliberate than approving something flagged only for *amount*, keeps the checkpoint's behavior aligned with the actual risk it exists to catch.

Every one of those four decisions — what triggers the gate, what context it shows, what options it offers, and how its interaction is weighted — is a context management decision in the sense this module has used throughout: a deliberate choice about what a reader (here, a human) sees and doesn't see at the moment they have to decide.

**Why it matters for the exam:** Expect scenario questions describing a review gate and asking you to evaluate whether it's well designed, often by presenting a checkpoint that technically exists but hands the reviewer either too little context to decide well or so much that a careful decision becomes impractical. The correct diagnosis usually isn't "add a review gate" or "remove the review gate" — it's "the gate exists but its context is poorly scoped," which is a distinctly Context Layer failure even though the checkpoint itself is a workflow-level construct.
