# Extended Thinking: When and How to Use It
{: .no_toc }

*Video duration: 12:00*

The previous lecture covered chain-of-thought as a prompting technique — asking a model, through ordinary instructions, to reason step by step within its normal response. **Extended thinking** is a different thing entirely: it's a built-in capability of Claude models, exposed directly through the API, in which the model is given a dedicated reasoning budget to work through a problem before producing its final response, using a mode the API treats distinctly from ordinary output generation rather than something achieved purely by prompt phrasing. Understanding the difference — and knowing when the added latency and cost of extended thinking is actually worth paying for — is a distinct architectural skill from writing a good prompt.

## What Extended Thinking Actually Is

When extended thinking is enabled for a request, the model is allotted a thinking budget — a token allowance dedicated to working through the problem — and it uses that budget to reason before generating the response that's ultimately returned as the answer. This differs from prompted chain-of-thought in a meaningful way: prompted chain-of-thought reasoning is ordinary output, generated with the same mechanism as any other text the model produces, and it consumes the same output budget as everything else in the response. Extended thinking is a mode the API surfaces as its own concept, with the reasoning content typically returned in its own distinct block, separately from the final answer, and governed by its own budget rather than being interleaved as regular prose the developer had to explicitly ask for and structure themselves.

Practically, this means extended thinking gives an architect a dial that prompted chain-of-thought doesn't: you can allocate a specific reasoning budget to a request and let the model use as much or as little of it as the problem actually demands, rather than relying entirely on prompt wording to coax out an appropriate amount of visible reasoning. It also means the reasoning Claude produces under extended thinking is treated architecturally as a first-class part of the response — something your system can inspect, log, and reason about deliberately — rather than an incidental artifact of how you happened to phrase the prompt.

## When a Task's Difficulty Justifies It

Extended thinking earns its cost on the same category of problem that benefits from chain-of-thought in general — multi-step reasoning, problems with many interacting constraints, and tasks that require weighing several plausible interpretations before committing to one — but it's specifically suited to the *harder* end of that spectrum, where the reasoning required is substantial enough that giving the model a large, dedicated space to work through it produces meaningfully better results than a modestly prompted "think step by step" would. Complex multi-constraint planning (scheduling a set of tasks against several competing resource limits), difficult mathematical or logical derivations, and subtle judgment calls that require weighing many pieces of evidence against each other before reaching a conclusion are the kinds of tasks where extended thinking's larger, purpose-built reasoning budget tends to show its value most clearly.

Conversely, a task that's already reliably solved by a direct prompt, or one that only requires a short, linear chain of two or three logical steps, generally doesn't need extended thinking's larger budget — a well-structured prompted chain-of-thought, at a fraction of the latency and cost, will get you there just as reliably. The judgment call an architect has to make is essentially the same cost/benefit exercise that runs through this entire module: what does the task actually require, and does the marginal accuracy gain from a bigger reasoning budget justify the marginal cost? Extended thinking is not a general-purpose "make it better" switch to flip on every request by default — it's a tool reserved for problems whose difficulty genuinely warrants it.

## How It Interacts with Tool Use

Extended thinking becomes especially relevant in agentic systems that combine reasoning with tool calls, because the model may need to reason not just about the final answer but about *which tool to call, with what arguments, and why* — a decision that itself can be a nontrivial multi-step judgment call. In an agentic loop, the model might need to reason through which of several available tools is appropriate for a given sub-task, what information is still missing before a call can be made correctly, and how to interpret an ambiguous or partial tool result before deciding on the next action. Extended thinking gives the model space to work through that reasoning explicitly before committing to a tool call, which matters because a poorly reasoned tool call — the wrong tool, malformed arguments, a premature call made before sufficient information was gathered — can be considerably more expensive to recover from than a wrong answer in a single-turn text response, since it may trigger a real side effect (a database write, an email sent, a payment processed) rather than just producing text a human or downstream system can simply discard and retry.

This means extended thinking is frequently most valuable not on the "final answer" step of an agentic task but on the intermediate decision points — the moments where the model is deciding how to proceed rather than what to ultimately conclude. An architect designing an agentic system should think about where in the loop reasoning quality actually matters most, and consider whether those specific decision points — rather than the entire loop uniformly — are where the added budget of extended thinking is worth allocating.

## When a Simpler Prompt-Based Approach Is Enough

Given the added latency and cost, the default assumption should not be "turn on extended thinking for everything that touches reasoning." For a large share of tasks in a production system — routine classification, well-defined extraction, straightforward single-step decisions — a clearly written prompt, possibly with a modest amount of prompted chain-of-thought for tasks that need it, will produce reliable results without the overhead of a dedicated reasoning mode. The signal that a task has outgrown a simpler approach is usually empirical rather than theoretical: if you're seeing a meaningful error rate on a task that genuinely involves multi-step or high-ambiguity reasoning, and prompt refinement plus prompted chain-of-thought haven't closed the gap, that's the point at which testing extended thinking against the same task becomes worth the engineering time.

It's also worth remembering that latency compounds in user-facing and agentic contexts specifically — a single extra second of reasoning time is a very different cost in a batch pipeline processing thousands of records overnight than it is in a live chat interface where a user is waiting on the response, or in an agentic loop where that reasoning delay recurs on every single step of a multi-step task. Part of choosing when extended thinking is appropriate is being honest about where in your system the added latency actually lands, and whether the consumers of that latency — a human waiting live, or a batch job with no one watching the clock — can absorb it without degrading the experience or the economics of the system.

## A Worked Comparison

Consider an agentic system that triages incoming infrastructure incidents: given a stream of alerts, logs, and recent deployment history, it decides whether an incident is a false alarm, a known issue with a documented fix, or a novel problem that needs to page a human on-call engineer. For the first category — matching an alert against a list of known false-alarm signatures — a direct prompt with no special reasoning mode is entirely sufficient; the task is close to a lookup, and adding a dedicated reasoning budget would add latency without changing the outcome.

The third category is where extended thinking earns its cost. Deciding whether an incident is genuinely novel requires weighing several pieces of evidence against each other — does this log pattern resemble a known issue closely enough to count as a match, or is the resemblance superficial; does the timing correlate with a recent deployment in a way that suggests a causal link; is the severity consistent with what the alert itself claims, or does it look overstated or understated relative to the underlying signals. Getting this judgment wrong in either direction is costly: paging a human unnecessarily erodes trust in the system, while failing to page for a genuinely novel incident delays a real fix. Giving the model a larger, dedicated reasoning budget for exactly this decision point — while leaving the false-alarm-matching step on a simple, low-latency direct prompt — is a good illustration of the principle from the tool-use section above: extended thinking is best allocated to the specific decision points where reasoning quality is load-bearing, not applied uniformly across every step an agentic system takes.

**Why it matters for the exam:** Expect questions distinguishing extended thinking, a built-in API capability with its own dedicated reasoning budget, from prompted chain-of-thought, a technique achieved purely through prompt instructions using ordinary output tokens — this distinction is tested directly, not just implied. Also expect scenario questions asking whether a described task's difficulty and stakes justify extended thinking's added cost and latency, versus scenarios where a simpler, well-structured prompt is clearly sufficient and extended thinking would be unnecessary overhead.
