# Trade Risk Management/Compliance - Observability and Costs
{: .no_toc }

*Video duration: 11:44*

Observability in a regulated domain answers a different question than observability anywhere else in this course. For the idea generation system, the goal of monitoring was mostly to make the system better over time — catching drift, tracking whether ideas panned out, keeping analysts engaged rather than complacent. Here, observability has an additional, non-negotiable purpose: the system has to be able to prove, to an internal auditor or an external regulator, exactly what it did, why, based on what information, and who ultimately approved it. A trade risk and compliance system that works well but can't produce that record hasn't actually solved the business problem — auditability isn't a feature layered on top of this system, it's part of the specification.

## Full Decision Logging as a Requirement, Not a Feature

Every review this system performs generates a permanent, immutable log entry, and that entry needs to capture considerably more than "trade approved" or "trade flagged." A complete record includes: the trade details as submitted, the full set of deterministic checks that ran and their pass/fail results, the compliance review agent's complete input context (which rules were retrieved, at which versions, with which effective dates), the agent's full structured output (which rules it judged applicable, its confidence, its severity classification, and its reasoning), the escalation path the trade took if any, the identity and decision of every human reviewer who touched it, and a timestamp for every step. This is substantially more logging discipline than a typical production system carries, and that's intentional — the standard here isn't "enough to debug an incident," it's "enough to reconstruct, months later, a defensible account of exactly why this trade was allowed to execute."

A subtlety worth calling out explicitly: the log has to capture the *rule versions actually applied*, not just a reference to "current rules," precisely because rules change and a log entry that resolves against today's rule text rather than the rule text in force at review time silently corrupts the historical record. This is the observability-layer consequence of the versioning discipline covered in the retrieval architecture lecture — if retrieval doesn't preserve rule versions, no amount of logging can recover them after the fact. The log entry, the retrieved rule versions, and the agent's citations all need to agree with each other, and periodic internal audits should specifically check that agreement rather than assuming it holds because the system was built correctly once.

This logging has to be genuinely immutable — append-only, with no code path (including administrative ones) that edits or deletes a past review record — because the value of an audit trail is entirely a function of its trustworthiness, and a log that can be quietly edited after the fact isn't an audit trail, it's a liability. Where the idea generation system could reasonably prune or summarize old logs to save storage, this system generally cannot; retention requirements in regulated environments are often measured in years, and the cost of storage is trivial compared to the cost of being unable to produce a required record.

## Tracking Accuracy and False Negatives

Unlike idea generation, where "was the thesis right" takes months to know, this system gets faster (though still imperfect) feedback: did a trade that the system judged low-risk turn out to have a compliance issue that surfaced later, whether through an internal audit, a counterparty dispute, or regulatory inquiry? Every such case is a **false negative** — the highest-priority failure mode to track, because a missed compliance issue is categorically worse than an over-cautious flag that turns out to be unnecessary. False negatives should be tracked individually, root-caused (was the relevant rule not indexed? was it indexed but not retrieved? was it retrieved but misjudged as inapplicable? was it correctly flagged but the escalation path failed to route it?), and fed back into both the retrieval system and the agent's evaluation criteria.

**False positives** — trades flagged for review that a senior compliance officer determines were actually fine — matter too, but differently. Some rate of false positives is the expected and healthy cost of a system tuned to be conservative; a false-positive rate of literally zero would be a warning sign that the system isn't flagging enough, not a sign of excellent precision. What's worth tracking is whether the false-positive rate is *concentrated* — the same rule or the same type of trade generating repeated unnecessary flags points to a genuine retrieval or prompt issue worth fixing, both because it wastes reviewer time and because reviewers who get worn down by repetitive noise are more likely to start rubber-stamping flags in that category, which is exactly the complacency risk that a conservative-by-design system is supposed to guard against, not accidentally cause.

## Human Override and Escalation Metrics

As with the idea generation system, override behavior is one of the most informative signals available, but it needs a different read here because of the asymmetric cost of the two error types. Tracking how often senior reviewers overturn a standard-queue clearance (finding an issue the agent missed) is the closest available proxy for the false-negative rate discussed above, and any sustained non-zero rate here deserves real scrutiny, not just a note in a monthly report. Tracking how often reviewers clear a flag the agent raised is expected and healthy in moderate volume, but — as with the idea generation system — a steadily falling rate of substantive engagement with flags (measured, for instance, by how quickly flags get cleared, or whether cleared flags show evidence of a full review versus a rubber stamp) is a complacency signal worth acting on before it becomes a real miss.

Escalation-path metrics deserve their own tracking separate from general override rates: how often the mandatory hard-coded escalation triggers actually fire, how long escalated items sit before senior review, and — most importantly — whether there's ever a case where an escalation should have triggered by policy but didn't. That last case is a system integrity failure, not a normal operating variance, and it should be treated with the same urgency as a security incident: it means the routing logic that's supposed to guarantee human review in high-severity cases has a gap, and until that gap is understood and closed, every review the system has performed since the gap was introduced needs to be considered for re-review.

## Cost Management Under Low Tolerance for Cutting Corners

Cost optimization in this domain operates under a constraint the idea generation system didn't have: nearly every lever that saves money in a typical system — using a cheaper model, skipping a retrieval pass, batching for throughput instead of reviewing individually — directly trades against review depth, and review depth is the one thing this domain has the least room to compromise on. That doesn't mean cost is unmanageable, but it means the optimization has to happen in places that don't touch the quality of the actual compliance judgment.

**Model tiering still applies, but conservatively.** The deterministic tier costs almost nothing regardless of volume, since it's ordinary code with no model calls — this is actually the system's biggest structural cost advantage over a hypothetical design that ran everything through an agent, and it's a reminder that keeping mechanical checks out of the agentic tier isn't just safer, it's also cheaper. Within the agentic tier itself, though, there's little appetite for a cheap-model fast path the way idea generation's screening stage had one; every trade that reaches the compliance review agent is, by definition, one that already needs judgment, and running that judgment on a weaker model to save cost directly increases false-negative risk in exactly the place this domain can least afford it. If tiering is used at all here, it should be based on trade complexity or notional size — larger or more complex trades always get the strongest available model — never used as a blanket cost-cutting measure across the board.

**Caching is a strong, low-risk lever.** The regulatory and policy corpus, while it changes, changes far less often than it's queried — the same rule text gets retrieved across many trades in a given period, and caching the retrieved rule content (with cache invalidation strictly tied to version changes, never time-based alone) saves real cost without touching review quality at all, since the actual reasoning still runs fresh against that cached, correctly current context every time.

**Batch versus real-time is more constrained here than in idea generation.** Some trades genuinely need review before execution in something close to real time, particularly for time-sensitive strategies, and that latency requirement isn't something cost optimization gets to override. Where batching legitimately helps is in lower-urgency categories — periodic look-back reviews of already-cleared trades, bulk re-review triggered by a rule change, or overnight processing of the next day's anticipated trade flow for strategies where positions are known in advance. The discipline here is being honest about which reviews are genuinely latency-sensitive and which aren't, rather than defaulting everything to real-time processing out of caution and paying for synchronous compute the task didn't actually need.

## Architecture Notes

Immutable, version-linked decision logging is the Context layer's provenance discipline taken to its logical conclusion: in a regulated domain, provenance isn't just what makes an output trustworthy in the moment, it's what makes a past decision reconstructable under audit, potentially years later. Cost discipline here is bounded by the Agentic layer's autonomy decisions from earlier in this module — because the deterministic tier carries the bulk of the volume at near-zero marginal cost, the system's total cost profile is favorable precisely because mechanical checks were kept out of the agentic path, proving that the safest architectural choice and the cheapest one aligned rather than traded off against each other.
