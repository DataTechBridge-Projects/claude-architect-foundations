# Trade Risk Management/Compliance - Overview
{: .no_toc }

*Video duration: 6:24*

Every proposed trade at a hedge fund passes through a review layer before it executes: does this trade breach a position limit, does it create prohibited concentration, does it touch a restricted list, does it comply with the regulatory obligations that apply to this fund, this strategy, and this counterparty? That review has historically been a mix of automated hard-coded checks (position limits are usually already enforced by rigid systems) and human judgment calls for anything the hard-coded checks don't cleanly cover — ambiguous restricted-list matches, trades that touch multiple overlapping rules, unusual structures that don't map neatly onto a pre-built check. The business problem this use case addresses is that second category: the judgment-requiring edge of compliance and risk review, where the volume of proposed trades and the complexity of applicable rules has grown faster than the compliance team reviewing them, and where a Claude-based system can help by reading rules and trade details together and surfacing what a human reviewer needs to look at closely — without ever being the thing that clears a trade.

This is a genuinely different kind of use case from idea generation, and it's worth being explicit about why. Idea generation is a creativity-and-synthesis problem where the cost of a mediocre output is a few minutes of an analyst's time; a bad idea that gets caught costs almost nothing. Trade risk and compliance review is the opposite: it's a **high-stakes, low-autonomy-tolerance** problem, where the cost of a missed violation isn't measured in wasted attention but in real financial loss, regulatory exposure, and — in a genuinely bad case — legal and reputational damage to the fund. That asymmetry changes the entire architecture. A system in this domain earns trust not by being clever or by handling ambiguous cases gracefully, but by being conservative, transparent, and easy to audit — three properties that are frequently in tension with the properties that make an agentic system feel powerful and autonomous.

## Why Low Autonomy Tolerance Shapes Everything

The core architectural tension in this domain is that compliance and risk rules are simultaneously rule-based *and* genuinely ambiguous. A position limit is a hard number — a trade either breaches it or it doesn't, and that check is deterministic and shouldn't involve a language model's judgment at all. But many of the rules that matter most in practice aren't that clean: a restricted-list match might be a near-miss on a similar name, a counterparty's regulatory status might depend on how a recent filing is interpreted, a trade structure might trigger two rules that point in different directions. It's precisely this ambiguous middle ground — not the clean deterministic checks and not fully open-ended judgment — where a Claude-based system adds real value, by reading the actual rule text, the actual trade details, and the actual counterparty context together and surfacing a reasoned flag for a compliance officer, rather than either silently passing an ambiguous case through or requiring a human to manually reconstruct the same reading from scratch every time.

Because the domain has essentially zero tolerance for a confidently wrong "this trade is fine," the system's design goal is not to maximize how much it can decide on its own — it's to maximize how much useful, well-sourced analysis it can hand a human reviewer before that human still has to sign off. This is the opposite instinct from a lot of agentic system design, where the goal is often to push autonomy as far as the task allows. Here, the task allows very little autonomy by design, not by current technical limitation, and a well-built system treats that as a permanent constraint rather than something to be relaxed as the model improves.

## The High-Level Shape of the Solution

The solution separates cleanly into two tiers that should never be confused with each other. A **deterministic tier** handles the checks that are genuinely mechanical — position limits, notional caps, hard restricted-list matches, and other rules expressible as a clean function of trade data — using ordinary rule-evaluation code with no model involvement at all, because a language model is strictly worse than a lookup table at enforcing a hard numeric limit. An **agentic tier** sits alongside it and handles the judgment-requiring cases: reading the applicable rule text, the trade's specific details, and relevant counterparty or historical context to produce a structured assessment — what rules might apply, why, how confident the assessment is, and what a human reviewer should check next. Every output from the agentic tier is explicitly a recommendation with citations back to the specific rule text and trade details it relied on, routed to a compliance officer for the actual clearance decision.

The lectures that follow this one dig into each layer of that design: how autonomy boundaries and mandatory escalation are structured at the agentic layer, what context a review needs and how conflicting or stale rules are handled, how retrieval over compliance documents is versioned so the system always cites the rule it actually applied, and how a regulated domain's auditability requirements shape observability and cost decisions that would look excessive anywhere else in this course.

## Architecture Notes

This use case is where the course's general five-layer vocabulary meets its sharpest real-world constraint: the Agentic layer's "how much autonomy should this system have" question has an answer here that's fixed by the domain, not by model capability — very little, and never over execution. The split between a deterministic tier and an agentic tier is a Workflow-layer decision made explicit: not every step in a review process should be agentic just because an agentic system is available, and the discipline of keeping mechanical checks mechanical is itself part of good architecture.
