# LPA/Subscription Agreements - RAG & Retrieval Architecture
{: .no_toc }

*Video duration: 8:52*

Retrieval for subscription documents looks different from retrieval for credit agreements in one important respect: it isn't primarily about searching a large corpus of past deals to find comparable language. It's about checking a single, well-defined document — the fund's own governing LPA — against every subscription document that references it, to make sure nothing an investor is agreeing to actually conflicts with what the fund's constitutional document says. That's a narrower, more targeted retrieval problem than credit agreement precedent search, but it comes with its own precision requirements.

## The retrieval target: one governing document, checked repeatedly

Unlike credit agreements, where the comparison corpus is a sprawling precedent library across many past deals, a subscription document consistency check has a single, fixed reference document per fund: that fund's own LPA. This matters architecturally because it changes the retrieval problem from "find the most relevant precedent among thousands of candidates" to "find the specific provisions of one known document that are relevant to this specific subscription clause" — a much more tractable, and much more precision-demanding, task. There's no ambiguity about *which* document to search; the only question is which *sections of it* are relevant to a given check.

That said, the LPA itself is a long, structured legal document in the same family as a credit agreement — it has its own defined-terms article, its own provisions on capital commitments, transfer restrictions, distribution waterfalls, governance and advisory committee rights, and indemnification — and it benefits from the same clause-level, structure-aware indexing described in the credit agreements module. The LPA is chunked by its own legal architecture (definitions, capital contribution mechanics, distribution provisions, transfer and withdrawal rights, GP/LP governance), and each chunk is tagged with what topic and section it corresponds to, so that a consistency check against a specific subscription clause can retrieve exactly the LPA provisions that govern that topic rather than searching the document indiscriminately.

## What triggers a retrieval, and what gets retrieved

A subscription document consistency check is really a set of narrower checks, each of which maps to a specific slice of the LPA. When a subscription document specifies a commitment amount and structure, the relevant retrieval target is the LPA's capital commitment and drawdown provisions — checking, for instance, that the commitment structure the investor is agreeing to is consistent with the fund's stated minimum commitment and drawdown mechanics. When a subscription document includes representations about transferability or withdrawal, the retrieval target is the LPA's transfer restriction and withdrawal provisions. When it includes side letter references or investor-specific governance rights (say, an advisory committee seat), the retrieval target is the LPA's governance and side-letter-acknowledgment provisions, which typically specify what kinds of investor-specific arrangements the LPA itself permits the GP to grant.

This mapping — clause type in the subscription document to provision type in the LPA — plays the same architectural role that the deal-type template played in the credit agreements module: it's a known, predetermined mapping that tells the retrieval step exactly what to look for, rather than leaving the model to search the LPA freely for anything that might be relevant. That predetermined mapping is what keeps the consistency check precise: a free-text semantic search over the whole LPA for "anything related to this subscription clause" would surface plausible-looking but wrong provisions far more often than a targeted retrieval that already knows a commitment-amount check belongs in the capital contributions article.

## Precision requirements: false conflicts are as costly as missed ones

Consistency checking against a governing document has an asymmetric error cost that's worth naming explicitly. A **missed conflict** — the system fails to notice that a subscription document's terms actually contradict the LPA — is a genuine legal risk, potentially binding the fund to inconsistent obligations. But a **false conflict** — the system flags a consistency issue that isn't actually one, because retrieval pulled an LPA provision that doesn't really govern the clause in question, or because the model misread a qualification that resolves the apparent tension — has a real cost too: it burns fund counsel's time reviewing non-issues, and if it happens often enough, it teaches reviewers to stop trusting the flags altogether, which quietly defeats the whole point of having the check.

Both error modes point to the same design requirement: retrieval has to be precise, not just broadly relevant, and every flagged inconsistency has to come with a clear citation to both the specific subscription document clause and the specific LPA section it's being checked against, so a human reviewer can immediately see the actual language on both sides rather than trusting an assertion that a conflict exists. This is the same citation discipline as the credit agreements module, applied to a two-document comparison instead of a document-versus-precedent comparison — the format of "show your work" is the same even though the retrieval target is different.

## Scaling across many documents without re-deriving the same retrieval

Because a single fund closing might run a consistency check against the same LPA for two hundred different subscription documents, there's a meaningful efficiency opportunity that doesn't exist in the credit agreements case, where every document being checked has its own distinct precedent comparison set. Here, the reference document — the LPA — is constant across the entire batch. The practical design is to pre-index and pre-chunk the LPA once per fund, at the start of a closing, rather than re-processing it for every subscription document that needs to be checked against it. Each individual consistency check then only needs to run the lightweight retrieval step (map the subscription clause type to the relevant pre-indexed LPA section) rather than re-parsing and re-chunking the LPA from scratch two hundred times over. This is a direct consequence of the volume-driven nature of this use case discussed in the overview: the retrieval architecture should be built to amortize the one genuinely expensive step — understanding and indexing the LPA — across the entire batch of documents that reference it, which is exactly the kind of design decision that separates a system that scales linearly with a fund's LP count from one that scales sublinearly.

**Architecture Notes:** This is retrieval scoped by a Workflow-layer mapping — clause type to LPA provision type — that plays the same role the deal-type template played for credit agreements, keeping the search targeted rather than open-ended. The asymmetric cost of false versus missed conflicts is a Context-layer concern about provenance and citation: every flagged inconsistency needs traceable evidence from both documents, because an unsupported flag erodes trust as surely as a missed one creates risk.
